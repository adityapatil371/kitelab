Why nothing passes the gate

Four diagnostics on the strict-majority walk-forward check. The gate is not too strict — it is a coin flip that ought to be passing hundreds of cells. It passes almost none because the rules genuinely lose to owning the same stocks.

scripts.wf_ceiling

The gate is passable. Nothing passes it.

The first suspicion was that the check was rigged against every rule — that a strict majority of seven three-year windows against each window's own equal-weight hold was unpassable by construction. It is not.

A hindsight-perfect selector — one that, in each window independently, is handed whichever of the 190 cells turned out best — clears the majority in every universe. So the bar can be reached. But look at the slack: in all and large, that omniscient selector wins exactly the four windows it needs and not one more. Only four of the seven windows were ever won by any cell on the board.

On the 22. Every passing cell sits in recent, whose stocks did not exist before 2018, so it offers three comparable windows and "strict majority" means two of three. An exact sign-flip test on two-of-three returns p = 0.375. These are not passes; they are the smallest sample on the board behaving like a small sample.

Ruled out

Cash drag is not the explanation. The suspicion that a partly-invested rule cannot beat an always-invested benchmark does not survive measurement: across 380 grid cells per universe from 2018, median exposure is 98.1–99.8% of days invested and the median account holds 0.0–0.8% in cash. These accounts are effectively fully invested. The shortfall is which stocks are held and when — not money left idle.

scripts.wf_excess

Not near misses. Blowouts.

The gate throws away almost everything it is given: each window collapses to a single win/lose bit. Replacing that bit with the actual margin — rule CAGR minus hold CAGR, in percentage points per year — changes the character of the result completely. The rules are not sitting just under the line.

Across all 5,820 comparable window comparisons the median rule trails equal-weight buy-and-hold by 17.8 points of CAGR per year. Only 2.6% of comparisons land within two points of the benchmark, while 69.0% trail it by more than ten.

scripts.wf_power

The gate passes half of all worthless rules

"Wins a strict majority of seven windows" is a sign test whose critical value sits at the median of its own null. Under a coin-flip null, P(X ≥ 4) = 64/128 — exactly one half. This is not a 5% test; it is a 50% test.

To measure it rather than assert it: draw seven window margins from a normal with a known true edge and the board's own noise — the median within-cell standard deviation of the real margins is 15.2 points per year, across the 740 cells with a full seven-window history — then apply three designs to identical draws, 20,000 rules per edge size.

The instrument problem

Seven numbers cannot support a test. At this noise level you may have a 50% false-positive rate, or you may have a test that needs a 20-point annual edge before it notices. There is no threshold that gives you neither — the sample size forbids it.

And that is what makes the headline result so stark. At a 50% pass rate, roughly 378 of the 760 cells with a full seven-window history should have passed on luck alone. Zero did. In recent, ~95 of 190 were expected and 22 arrived. Systematic underperformance on this scale is far harder to achieve by accident than passing would have been.

scripts.wf_cost_gap

The cost bug is real, and it is worth 0.09 points

One known defect could in principle have manufactured this whole result: the cell's CAGR is computed net of costs while equal-weight hold is computed gross of them. The rule pays the Zerodha schedule on every round trip; the benchmark has never paid a rupee. Every margin on the board is biased against the rule.

It needs no rebuild to price. Buy-and-hold buys each member once and sells once, so the fee is a single multiplicative haircut on that member's terminal wealth — identical across members and independent of the price path. Net wealth is gross wealth times a constant, so the corrected CAGR follows in closed form from figures the dashboard already carries.

Correcting the benchmark. Proportional delivery charges (STT, exchange transaction, SEBI, stamp duty, GST) read live from kitelab.backtest: 0.1187% on the buy, 0.1037% on the sell. Spread is swept because the fill grid axis can switch it on.

conclusions

What follows

The walk-forward gate was suspected of being an unfair instrument. It is an unfair instrument — but in the opposite direction to the one assumed, and its unfairness flatters rules rather than condemning them. Correcting it will not rescue the board.

  1. Stop treating a walk-forward failure as a threshold problem

    No redesign of the majority rule recovers these strategies. The best cell on the whole board, in the universe with the most data, still averages −4.8 points a year against simply owning the same stocks. The gate is not what stands between these rules and a pass.

  2. Replace seven windows with daily excess returns

    The binding constraint is sample size, not threshold choice. Going to daily rule-minus-hold returns replaces 7 observations with roughly 5,000, which is what makes an honest 5% test possible at a detectable effect size. This is the one change with real leverage — and it needs a stored daily hold curve, which does not exist yet.

  3. Fix the net-vs-gross asymmetry anyway

    It is worth 0.09 points a year, but it is a genuine defect in a comparison the whole project rests on, and leaving a known bias in place because it happens to be small is how small biases survive into contexts where they are not.

  4. Report the margin, not the win count

    The win/lose bit discards the information that mattered most here. "Wins 3 of 7" and "trails by 22 points a year" are the same cell; only one of them tells you what happened.

Scope

These are in-sample figures on a survivorship-biased universe — the roughly 4.9 points a year of survivorship bias documented in the project notes sits inside both the rule and the benchmark, so it does not create the gap, but no number here is a forecast. Independence across windows is assumed in the power simulation, and it is an optimistic assumption: real windows share a market factor, so true power is somewhat worse than reported.
